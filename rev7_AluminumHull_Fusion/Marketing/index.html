Picture it

A Saturday, eighteen months from now

5:50 a.m.

The truck is hitched before the coffee is done. The boat on the trailer behind it is one you riveted together yourself, every one of its 2,500 rivets. Your neighbor has stopped asking what you were doing in that garage all last summer.

6:25 a.m.

West on Griffin Road, past the last of the subdivisions, to the ramp at the edge of the Glades. Mist is still sitting on the canal. You back it in, float it off and climb onto the bench.

6:40 a.m.

Key on. The fuel gauge you wired shows full. The junkyard Suzuki you rebuilt on an engine stand catches on the second crank, and the carbon prop you pitched with a protractor spins up behind you.

7:05 a.m.

Thirty miles an hour across the sawgrass with a friend on the back seat, egrets lifting off the water ahead. The cooler under the passenger cushion has breakfast in it.

7:30 p.m.

The run home is in the dark, so you switch on the light bar and swing the searchlight onto the channel markers from your seat. Back at the ramp, somebody asks where you bought it. You get to say my garage.

Cheaper every trip, too

A 1.3 L four-cylinder at cruise burns about 3.5–4.5 gallons an hour in the real world. Big V8 airboats burn 8–10. At about $3.90 a gallon that's roughly $14–18 an hour against $31–39.

And the parts are everywhere

The engine came in millions of Geo Metros and Suzuki Swifts, and the radiator is a Honda Civic part. The electrics use standard marine fuses and Deutsch connectors. When something wears out, you already know how to fix it, because you built it.

The hull, step by step

No welding. Just clecos, a drill and a rivet gun.

The hull is the part people assume they can't build, yet it's the one part that needs no welding at all. It goes together like a big sheet-metal kit: you hold it with clecos, drill it, take it apart, seal it and rivet it. Homebuilt-aircraft builders have built this way for decades. It takes about 150 of your hours.

1

Print the templates

6 hLaptop, printer, tape

Export the flat pattern of every skin panel, frame and stringer from the free CAD files. Print them full size and tape them together on the garage floor.

2

Build the strongback

10 h2×6 lumber, plywood, level

Build a ladder-style table with plywood station molds at each frame, shimmed level and flat to 1/16 in end to end. Everything after this step is only as straight as this table.

3

Let a fab shop bend the big pieces

4 h of yours≈ $600 shop service

Take your templates to a local sheet-metal shop. They shear the 5052 aluminum blanks and brake the long 45° chine bends on machines you don't need to own.

4

Make stringers and frames

19 hChop saw, files, drill

Bend the hat-section stringers to the gentle bow curve and cut the frames from aluminum angle. Notch a drain hole in every bay so water can always reach the sump.

5

Cleco the skeleton

14 hClecos, square, tape

Clecos are spring pins that hold metal together like temporary rivets. Pin the frames and stringers together on the jig, check the diagonals match, then rivet the framing.

6

Wrap it in skin

24 hClecos, hand seamer, snips

Lay the bottom sheets over the skeleton, then fit the sides, bow and transom with 1 in overlaps. It's all pinned with clecos for now, so nothing is permanent and every piece can still be adjusted.

7

Drill, seal, rivet for keeps

34 hPneumatic riveter, caulk gun

Drill about 2,000 holes, deburr them and pull the panels apart. Butter every overlap with marine sealant, then pull closed-end aluminum rivets with the air riveter, about two seconds each. In a 90°F Florida garage the sealant skins over fast, so work one seam at a time.

8

Engine beds and the hose test

16 hGarden hose, flashlight

Rivet in the engine beds and rub rails. Then fill the hull with 4 in of water from the garden hose and watch underneath for a day. Drill out and replace any rivet that weeps.

9

Wear strips and paint

22 hDrill, spray gun or rollers

Bolt the 1/4 in UHMW plastic strips under the bottom so the sawgrass and limestone wear them instead of your aluminum. Then prime and paint the hull. Now you own a boat hull.

The welded parts are small, bolt-on assemblies. You can cut and fit every piece yourself, then hand a shop the tacked assemblies to finish-weld, which costs far less than having them build it.

The space

It fits in a standard two-car garage

A 20 × 20 ft garage leaves about 6 ft of walk-around space on each side of the hull and nearly 4 ft at each end, with room for a bench and a sheet rack on one wall.

Build the hull, engine, bench and wiring inside. With the prop, cage and lights off, the boat on a low dolly stands about 6 ft 10 in at the radiator, so it rolls out under a standard 7 ft door with a couple of inches to spare. The cage and prop bolt on in the driveway.

Where it lives when it’s done

Finished and on its trailer, the boat is about 10½ ft tall to the stern light, even with the flag staff pulled out. It won't fit back in the garage, so plan for a tall carport, a side yard or a storage lot. Check your HOA's rules on trailers before you start.

Florida must-knows

Before your first run

Boater ID

Anyone born on or after January 1, 1988 needs a Florida Boating Safety Education ID card to run a boat of 10 hp or more.

Muffler

Florida law requires a real muffler on an airboat. The design has one, mounted above the aft flotation.

Orange flag

Florida law requires a 10 × 12 in international-orange flag at least 10 ft above the bottom of the boat. The design has a removable staff on the aft cage hoop that puts the flag's bottom edge at 10 ft 2 in.

Where to run

Everglades National Park is closed to private airboats. Broward locals run the Water Conservation Areas west of US-27, so check FWC's area rules and seasonal closures.

Who we’re talking to

The backyard mechanic with a free bay

Profile

Age 35–65, owns a house with a two-car garage in Davie, Weston, Southwest Ranches, Plantation or Coral Springs. Has swapped an engine, restored a Bronco or built an off-road rig. Some savings set aside for a toy, around $20–25k. Has time on weeknights and weekends, or is newly retired.

What they want

To be out in the Glades on their own boat rather than paying $40 a seat for a tour. A project with an ending. To be the person at the ramp everyone wants to talk to. To ride with a kid, a buddy or a partner who helped build it.

What stops them

“I've never built a boat.” “I can't weld aluminum.” “What if it leaks?” “Where would I even keep it?” “Will my HOA freak out?” “What if I quit halfway?”

Also in reach: Florida Python Challenge hunters; homebuilt-aircraft builders (they already rivet); parent-and-teen project teams; marine-tech and welding students who want a portfolio piece.

Risks

Overpromising

Label speed and fuel numbers as estimates. Say plainly that nobody has built one yet, and use that as the founding-builder hook.

Safety

Airboats can flip in hard turns at speed, and a prop cage can't contain a failed blade. Show safe operation in every shot and point builders to a boating course.

Noise and neighbors

Lead with the muffler and the 4-cylinder, and run engine tests at the ramp, not in the driveway at 6 a.m.

Abandoned builds

Milestone check-ins, the tool library, and a forum where an unfinished hull can find a new owner.
